# Charter and boundary standard

Use this reference to define or test a small, autonomous mission team.

## One-mission test

A useful mission answers five questions:

1. Who is the customer or beneficiary?
2. What durable problem does the team own?
3. What observable outcome should improve?
4. What work is explicitly outside the team's boundary?
5. Which authorized human changes the mission?

If the answers point to unrelated customers, outcomes, or decision owners, the charter likely contains more than one mission.

## Small, sufficient core

Team size is an output of the mission, not a target by itself. Include a capability in the dedicated core when at least one condition is true:

- the mission needs that capability for frequent delivery decisions;
- delays in that capability repeatedly block learning or operation;
- the team cannot meet a guardrail without it;
- an external interface would require constant negotiation.

Keep a role outside the core when periodic consultation, a written standard, or a service agreement can provide the needed input. The goal is not exclusion. It is to distinguish ongoing ownership from consultation and approval.

## Commitment test

Record actual allocation when supplied. Treat allocation as unknown when it is not supplied. Warning signs include:

- every core member has several equal priorities;
- the team's work can be interrupted without a named tradeoff decision;
- a critical capability has no committed capacity;
- the roster exists, but the mission has no protected planning horizon.

Partial commitment is a design risk, not a character judgment.

## Boundary design

Write both sides of the boundary:

- In scope: outcomes and recurring work the team owns.
- Out of scope: adjacent work another team or decision owner retains.
- Boundary test: a short question that classifies a new request.
- Change rule: who may change scope and what evidence is required.

A boundary is incomplete when it lists work but not the outcome or authority behind it.

## Decision rights

For each recurring decision, identify:

- Accountable: the one role that decides within the approved boundary.
- Contributors: roles whose evidence or expertise is needed before the decision.
- Informed: people who need the record or result, but do not need to approve it.
- Escalation: the condition that moves the decision outside the team.

Do not turn every informed party into a contributor or every contributor into an approver.

## Dependency interface

For each inbound or outbound dependency, record the service or input, quality or response expectation, owner on each side, failure path, and review date. Prefer a stable interface over recurring negotiation.

## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | Observable signal | Useful repair |
|---|---|---|
| Partial commitment | Work repeatedly pauses for other priorities | Set allocation and a tradeoff authority |
| Missing capability | A recurring decision always leaves the team | Add coverage or define a dependable interface |
| Scope duplication | Two teams claim the same outcome or backlog | Clarify mission boundaries and one accountable decision |
| Approval drag | Routine choices require several sequential approvals | Move bounded authority into the team and set escalation thresholds |
| Large core | Most members are only occasionally relevant | Move advisory roles to explicit consultation points |
| Metric tunnel | One outcome improves while quality or trust degrades | Add guardrail measures and stop conditions |
